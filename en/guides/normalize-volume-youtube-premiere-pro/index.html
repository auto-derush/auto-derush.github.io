Normalize Volume for YouTube (-14 LUFS) in Premiere Pro

3 min read

YouTube turns down videos that are too loud. Here is how to aim for -14 LUFS in Premiere Pro and avoid surprises after upload.

What YouTube does with your volume

YouTube measures your video in LUFS (perceived loudness, not peak level). The reference is around -14 LUFS. If your video is louder, YouTube turns it down. If it is quieter, it stays quiet. You cannot leave that to the platform.

So the goal is simple: land near -14 LUFS, with peaks that stay under -1 dB. A voice at -16 or -13 LUFS is perfectly fine. Don't chase the exact decimal.

Normalizing to peak ("Normalize Max Peak") is not normalizing to LUFS. A clip can peak at 0 dB and still sound quiet. Always measure in LUFS.

The manual method in Premiere Pro

1. Finish the edit first

Setting levels on an edit that is still changing means doing it twice. Cut, place the music, and set the final level last. If the dead air is not trimmed yet, How to remove silences in Premiere Pro comes before this guide.

2. Balance the voice, then the music

The voice is your reference. Set it first, then put the music under it. Music that is too loud pushes the overall measurement up and forces you to pull the voice down.

  • Select the voice clip and open Audio Gain (shortcut G) to raise or lower it by a few dB.
  • Or open Window > Essential Sound, tag the clip as Dialogue, then use the Loudness section and its Auto-Match button. Check the result afterward: the default target is not necessarily -14 LUFS.
  • If the voice is uneven (one guest quiet, another loud), fix each clip separately before setting the whole mix.

3. Measure the overall level

Put a loudness meter on the Master track. Premiere offers metering effects such as Loudness Radar. Play the whole timeline and read the integrated value, the average over the full duration. Ignore the momentary reading: it never stops moving.

4. Close the gap

If you measure -19 LUFS, you are 5 dB short. Two ways to fix it:

  1. Raise the gain of all audio clips by the same amount (select them all, then G).
  2. Or adjust the gain of the track or submix that carries voice and music.

Measure again. Two passes are usually enough.

5. Protect the peaks

If the voice jumps a lot, a peak can go over 0 dB and distort. Add a limiter on the Master track with the ceiling near -1 dB. It is not there to squash the sound, only to catch the spikes.

Check before you export

  • Listen to the end of the video. A laugh or a louder outro track can push the measurement up.
  • Play it on phone earbuds. That is how most of your viewers will hear it.
  • Depending on your Premiere Pro version, the Audio tab in Export Settings may offer loudness normalization. If you use it, still measure the exported file once.

Common mistakes

  • Pushing to -9 or -10 LUFS thinking it will hit harder. YouTube turns the whole thing down and you only lose dynamics.
  • Normalizing every clip to the same level, ambience included. The edit sounds flat.
  • Measuring before the edit is locked. One added shot can change everything.
  • Fixing a noisy recording with gain. You raise the hiss too. For that, see How to improve interview audio in Premiere Pro.
